Part One The product no technical knowledge needed

1The problem we're solving

Finding a good engineer currently means one person scrolling search results for hours, and no record of why anyone looked promising.

Today, sourcing for a role looks like this: open a job board or a professional network, type in some keywords, scroll through hundreds of results, open the promising ones one at a time, and try to judge from a list of self-described skills whether someone can actually do the job. Then do it again next week, because there's no saved view — and again for the next role, from scratch.

Three things go wrong. It takes a long time. The judgement is based on what people say about themselves, which is the weakest available evidence. And when a candidate gets passed to a hiring manager, nobody can explain precisely why that person was picked over the ninety others.

2What the tool does

You paste in a job description. The tool reads it and shows you what it understood — required skills, experience range, locations — which you correct if it got anything wrong. You press Find candidates.

It then spends a few minutes searching the places where engineers leave a public trail of their actual work: code they've published, projects they've built, packages other developers use, technical writing, portfolios, programming-contest results. It reads what it finds, works out who these people are, and ranks them against your requirements.

You get a ranked list. For each person, you can see every single thing the tool believes about them, and click straight through to the page it came from.

01Paste the roleJob description in, plain text
02Confirm what it readYou check and correct
03Search public workA few minutes, runs on its own
04Gather the evidenceEvery claim keeps its source
05Rank against the roleSame rules every time
06You decideShortlist, maybe, not a fit

Two of the six steps are yours. The tool never contacts anyone, never decides who's in or out, and never hides why someone ranked where they did.

3The one idea that makes this better than a keyword search

There is a large difference between someone listing a skill and someone demonstrably having it. Every other tool treats those the same. This one doesn't.

Consider two candidates who both come up in a search for Python:

What we foundHow we label itWhat it's worth
"Python" appears in the skills list on their personal site Says so Weak. Anyone can type it. Counts, but only a little.
Fourteen published Python projects, 2,100 commits, most recent three weeks ago Proven Strong. This is work you can go and read.

The tool keeps these permanently separate. It never quietly averages them into a single number. On screen, Proven and Says so look completely different, and proven evidence counts for roughly three times as much in the ranking.

This is also why every claim carries a link and a short quote from the page it came from. If you want to check something, you're one click from the original.

Why this matters in practice

When a hiring manager asks "why this person?", the answer is on the screen, in specifics: "Eleven years of published Go, maintains a library with 4,000 users, active last month, based in Bangalore." Not "the algorithm scored them 87."

4What you'll actually use

Four screens. Deliberately four — every extra screen is somewhere to get lost.

Screen 1 · Your roles

A list of the roles you're working on, how many candidates each has found, how many you've shortlisted. One button: New role.

Screen 2 · New role

A box to paste the job description. Then a short form showing what the tool understood, so you can fix it before it searches — skills as removable tags, experience as two numbers, locations by name ("Delhi NCR", "Bangalore"), and how you feel about remote work in plain words. Nothing runs until you press the button.

Screen 3 · The shortlist

The screen you'll spend your time on. Candidates ranked best-first, each with a plain-word match rating instead of a score out of a hundred.

5What this will not do

Worth reading carefully. These are deliberate limits, not gaps we plan to close, and knowing them now avoids disappointment later.

It won'tWhy
Search LinkedIn or paid resume databases as its main source Resume databases sit behind expensive recruiter subscriptions, and automated collection from professional networks has been aggressively litigated. We use public work instead. We may try a small, careful, capped LinkedIn helper later — see §12.
Find every good engineer It finds engineers who leave public work. That correlates with being good, but it systematically misses excellent people who publish nothing. This trades breadth for confidence, on purpose. It complements your existing sourcing; it doesn't replace it.
Tell you someone's salary or notice period That information doesn't exist publicly anywhere. It comes from talking to the person. You can record it in the tool afterwards; the tool can't find it.
Work for non-engineering roles yet Sales, ops, marketing and finance leave almost no public evidence of skill. The same approach would perform badly. Engineering first; we'll reassess.
Contact anybody No automated emails, ever. The tool identifies people and shows you their public links. Outreach stays human, through channels the person chose to publish.
Decide who to hire, or reject anyone on its own It ranks and explains. Every judgement that matters is yours, and anyone it filtered out is always visible with the reason.
Fairness — a deliberate design constraint

The ranking is forbidden from using college name or tier, graduation year, age, gender, caste, religion, marital status, or anything derived from someone's name. Not merely hidden from the filters — the system does not collect them at all. This is enforced in the code with a fixed list of permitted inputs, so adding anything new is a visible, reviewable change rather than a quiet one.

The first version is intentionally narrow: one source, end to end, in front of a real recruiter on a real open role. If the candidates it produces aren't good, we've learned that in three weeks rather than three months, and very little is wasted.

7What we need from you

Which role will we test on first?
We need one genuinely open engineering role, and one recruiter willing to judge the output honestly. The test is simple: of the top ten, how many would you actually contact? Six or more and this works.
How many engineering roles do you run in a year?
This matters more than it sounds. If it's two, a saved-search checklist would serve you better than software, and we should say so now. If it's fifteen or more, this pays back quickly.
Who signs off on data handling?
We're storing information about named individuals. India's data-protection rules came fully into force in November 2025. Our approach is deliberately conservative — public self-published work only, deletion honoured on request, nothing kept past 180 days unless you're actively considering the person — but somebody senior needs to have read and approved that before we switch on the first source.
Whose LinkedIn account, if we try the LinkedIn helper?
If we test it, it runs inside one person's own browser, at human pace, capped at fifteen profiles a day. The real risk is that that individual's account gets restricted. Whoever volunteers should understand that clearly and agree to it. It's optional, and we'll drop it if it doesn't earn its place.
Part Two How it's built for engineers

8Architecture

A monolith and a worker against one Postgres database. The interesting decisions are about data shape and sourcing, not infrastructure.

FastAPI · Postgres 16 · httpx + selectolax · Playwright as a lazy fallback only · Jinja2 + HTMX · the Anthropic SDK. One Docker Compose, one VM. The job queue is a Postgres table read with FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED by a single worker process.

Deliberately absent, each considered and rejected for a team this size: Redis, Celery, Kafka, Elasticsearch, a vector database, a crawler fleet, and a single-page app. Re-adding any of them requires a written reason.

DecisionInstead ofBecause
Role-triggered search, cachedContinuous crawlingProfiles go stale, and holding data on people you aren't evaluating is a liability. A search is minutes and a few dollars — run it when it's needed.
Evidence as the primary tableAttributes on a candidate rowExplainability, per-claim freshness, and cascade-deletion for erasure all fall out of one decision.
Postgres as the queueRedis + CeleryRoughly 30 searches a month. SKIP LOCKED is sufficient and adds no operational surface.
Server-rendered HTMXReact / Next.jsFour screens, no build step, one deploy artifact.
Undefended sources firstAnti-bot arms racePublic APIs, personal sites and PDFs need no proxies, no CAPTCHA solving, no fingerprint spoofing. Choosing sources well deletes the entire problem.
Search API for discovery onlyScraping GoogleThe most-defended target on the internet, and the substitute costs about 20¢ per role. We use it as a sitemap, then fetch and parse every page ourselves.

9The evidence model

Every assertion about a person is one row:

ColumnHolds
claim_typeskill, title, employer, location, activity, availability, project…
tierartifact_backed · third_party_stated · self_reported
source_urlthe page it came from
snippeta verbatim excerpt supporting the claim
observed_atwhen we saw it
extractorwhich parser or prompt version produced it

Candidate attributes are derived from evidence, never stored beside it. Three invariants are enforced in code and tested:

  • No snippet, no claim. The snippet must be a literal substring of the source document. This is checked programmatically after extraction, and a row that fails is dropped rather than corrected. It is the guard against an AI inventing a fact.
  • Tiers never merge. They are weighted separately and badged separately all the way to the screen.
  • Deleting a candidate cascades. Erasure is a foreign key, not a procedure.

Identity resolution uses strong keys only — a platform login, a personal domain, a hashed email. One key belongs to exactly one person, enforced by a unique constraint. Anything weaker goes to a human review queue. Nothing auto-merges, because a false merge is silent and irreversible.

10Ranking

Hard gates first — missing must-have skill, wrong location, no recent activity — each failure recorded with its reason and visible in the UI. Then a plain weighted sum of five components, every one in the range 0–1, all five always present:

ComponentMeasures
skill_matchtier-weighted coverage of the required skills (artifact 1.0, third-party 0.6, self-reported 0.35)
skill_depthvolume × recency of artefact evidence per must-have skill, log-scaled
seniority_fitdistance from the target range, using estimated years — never parsed from a job title
activity_recencydecay on the most recent artefact
availabilitypublic availability signal; unknown scores 0, never negative

Weights live in a database row with a version, not in code. Every stored match records the scorer version and weights version, so a re-score is a new row and you can always reconstruct why something ranked where it did last Tuesday. Two runs over identical inputs produce byte-identical output; scoring.py is a pure function with no I/O and no clock, which is why its tests are cheap.

Fairness is enforced structurally: the scorer reads only from a FEATURE_ALLOWLIST constant, and the component keys are asserted equal to it on every write. A BANNED_SIGNALS set covers college, graduation year, age, name tokens and the protected characteristics — and those are excluded at extraction time, so they never enter the database at all.

No learning from hire outcomes. Recruiter decisions aggregate into proposed weight changes on an admin page that a human approves. Training a ranker on historical hires would faithfully reproduce whatever bias those hires contained.

11Where AI is used, and where it isn't

This is conventional software with four AI call-sites. It is not an agent. There is no autonomous loop.

The governing rule: a model may read unstructured text and emit structured data, or write prose a human will read. It may not decide, rank, merge, or do arithmetic.

AI doesAI never does
Read a job description into a draft spec — which a human confirmsCompute or adjust a score
Expand search queries with skill synonymsDecide whether two profiles are the same person
Turn an unstructured page into evidence rows against a strict schemaDecide whether a URL may be fetched
Write the plain-language explanation of a rankingInfer anything about a person that isn't stated on a page

Each call-site is fail-safe. A bad job-description parse shows the raw output next to an empty form. A malformed extraction writes zero rows and quarantines the document — never partial data. A rationale citing evidence that doesn't belong to the candidate is discarded, and the score, computed beforehand, is unaffected.

Extraction runs on Haiku 4.5 at temperature 0 — it's schema-filling, not reasoning — with a stronger model for the two low-volume judgement tasks. Prompts are versioned files in the repo, and changing one requires a version bump because extractor version is part of a unique key.

12Sourcing and the legal position

Every source implements one small adapter interface, so each is independently switchable and reports its own yield — how many candidates it found, how many survived the gates, how many a recruiter actually shortlisted. That number, not an opinion, decides whether a source stays.

What we learned checking this out

  • Job boards expose jobs, not candidates. Their resume databases are behind paid recruiter logins. Scraping the public side yields postings, which are useless for sourcing. This invalidated the original plan's primary source.
  • hiQ Labs did not win. The 2019 ruling narrowing the CFAA is often cited as protecting scrapers, but the case settled in December 2022 with a $500,000 judgment against hiQ, liability for trespass to chattels and misappropriation, a permanent injunction, and an order to destroy the scraped data and derived algorithms.
  • Proxycurl, the largest LinkedIn data provider at ~$10M ARR, shut down permanently in July 2025 after LinkedIn and Microsoft sued. The aggravating fact was operating hundreds of thousands of fake accounts to reach data behind the login wall. The founder's stated reason for folding: he couldn't afford to fight Microsoft even if he would have won.
  • GitHub's acceptable-use policy permits collection through its API but forbids using the data to send unsolicited email. So we identify and evaluate from it; we do not harvest addresses. Emails are stored as SHA-256 only.
  • India's DPDP Act §3(c)(ii) excludes personal data the person published themselves — which covers most of what we collect, as processed in the form it was made public. Two important limits: crawlable is not the same as exempt, and combining public data with non-public data can pull the whole record back into scope. That last point is the strongest technical argument against ever importing a purchased enrichment file.

Operating posture

No domain can be fetched without an enabled row in a source_policy table, and the fetch layer is the only path to the network — robots.txt, per-host rate limiting, daily caps and caching all live there. A cross-domain redirect re-enters the policy check. No residential proxies, no CAPTCHA solving, no fingerprint spoofing: a target that needs those is a target we drop.

The LinkedIn helper — approved, tightly scoped

Three modes, ascending risk. Snippet-only reads title, company and location from public search results without ever fetching a page. Paste lets a recruiter paste a profile they opened themselves. Attached browser drives the recruiter's own already-logged-in Chrome: never headless, never a burner or shared account, no stored credentials, one profile at a time, at least twenty seconds apart, fifteen a day, and it disables itself on the first authwall or challenge until a human re-enables it.

These limits live in code, not configuration. The rationale: the fake-account fleet is what draws a lawsuit; a person using their own session at human pace is a different risk class. The residual risk is real and lands on an individual — that person's account may be restricted — so whoever's account is used must agree explicitly. Yield metrics decide within a month whether it stays.

13Build plan

Milestone 1 is a thin vertical slice: one source, real shortlists, four screens. Nothing broadens until a recruiter has used it on a real open role.

MScopeDone when
1 Schema · fetch layer · skill and location canon · GitHub adapter · evidence writer · identity resolution · role intake · scoring · job queue · four screens · login A recruiter runs a real role, gets ≥20 candidates, and says the top ten are worth contacting
2 Web-search adapter (portfolios, public resumes, speaker lists, maintainer files) · Hugging Face · Codeforces · manual paste · AI-written rationales Finds candidates GitHub alone missed, and the yield metrics prove it
3 The LinkedIn helper, all three modes, shipped in order It can be switched off with one row update, and 30 days of yield data can answer whether it earns its risk
4 Observability · retention and erasure jobs · weekly re-search of standing roles · weight proposals Erasure verifiably removes evidence rows and cached page bodies

Testing: no live network in CI — every adapter runs against recorded fixtures. Property tests on the pure scorer. Repo-wide invariant tests asserting every snippet is present in its source and every component set matches the allowlist. One end-to-end run from job description to shortlist. No load tests, no browser end-to-end, no coverage thresholds — wrong tools at this size.

14Risks, stated plainly

RiskSeverityResponse
Coverage disappoints a recruiter expecting LinkedIn-scale breadthHigh / likelySet the expectation in the UI itself, before the first search runs. Position as a complement, not a replacement.
Seniority estimates skew low — publishing history isn't career lengthMediumDisplay as a floor ("9+ years of public work"), never a range implying we know the ceiling.
The availability signal turns out to be near-emptyLowMeasure the distribution during Milestone 1; drop the component if it's flat rather than let it add noise.
Extraction silently misses content — we test against fabrication, not omissionMediumHand-label ~30 pages as a recall baseline before Milestone 2.
The LinkedIn helper gets an individual's account restrictedMedium / acceptedNamed volunteer, informed consent, hard caps, self-disabling. Droppable.
Nobody uses it because it's one more toolHighThe only real mitigation is Milestone 1 being genuinely good. Which is why it's narrow.

And the honest one: a recruiter with GitHub's advanced search and thirty minutes can do one role by hand. This tool earns its keep on repeatability, the evidence trail, and running five roles at once — not on finding things a person couldn't. If you hire two engineers a year, a saved-search checklist beats this, and we should decide that now rather than after the build.
